Hey there, savvy internet user! Guess what? Scammers are getting sneakier than ever, and they're pulling out all the stops to trick you. They’re even impersonating the FBI with deepfake videos on social media. Here’s the lowdown on their latest schemes and how you can stay one step ahead.

### The Sneaky Scam Tactics

1. **Fake FBI Agents on Social Media:**
   Imagine being contacted by someone claiming to be an FBI agent on Facebook Messenger or Telegram. They might say they're here to help you recover funds you lost to a scam. Spoiler alert: They're not! They just want to scam you again.

2. **Deepfake FBI Leaders:**
   Scammers are using AI-generated videos to impersonate FBI personnel. These videos feature convincing fake FBI leaders urging you to file complaints on a fraudulent website that looks eerily similar to the real FBI IC3 site. But beware! This site is just a trap to steal your personal details.

### Protect Yourself from These Tricksters

- **IC3 Doesn’t Do Facebook:** The real Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) does not operate on social media or apps like Telegram. Any page claiming otherwise is a scam.
  
- **Spot the Fakes:** Always type `www.ic3.gov` directly into your browser rather than clicking on links from search engines or social media. Verify that the URL ends with `.gov`.

- **Double-Check the Details:** Scammers might use slightly altered names, URLs, or email addresses to fool you. If something seems off, it probably is!

- **Stay Skeptical of AI Videos:** If a video of a public figure seems questionable, check for tell-tale signs like weird hand movements or odd facial features. If in doubt, confirm its authenticity with reliable sources.

- **Guard Your Emotions:** Scammers play on fear and urgency. If a video or message makes you feel panicked, take a step back and verify its truthfulness before reacting.

### What to Do If You’ve Been Targeted

If you or someone you know falls for one of these scams, file a complaint with the real IC3 at `www.ic3.gov`. Provide as much detail as possible, including:

- Who contacted you and how.
- Any financial transactions made.
- A description of your interaction, including any instructions given by the scammer.

For seniors aged 60 and over needing assistance, the DOJ Elder Justice Hotline is available at 1-833-FRAUD-11 (or 833-372-8311).

Stay safe out there, and remember: if it sounds too good to be true (or too scary), it probably is!
